# English Lingua Grounding

What follows is the literature behind each `§en` provision, the term as its source uses it, and what the provision deliberately declines to take.

## §en1

The taxonomy is Halliday's **logico-semantic relations**, the system of expansion set out in the chapter on the clause complex: elaboration, extension, enhancement, alongside projection. Elaboration divides into exposition, exemplification and clarification, which is where `i.e.` and `e.g.` enter the mapping under their own names.

Three qualifications the provision does not have room for. The mapping is working guidance rather than a reproduction of the taxonomy: the colon is assigned elaboration as a genus while `i.e.` and `e.g.` are assigned two of its species, and "aside" belongs to no category in the system. The list is also partial by necessity, since enhancement has no punctuation realization at all: temporal, causal and conditional relations need a conjunction. A reader who knows the source will notice both and should not take the provision as a claim to reproduce it.

Three supporting threads stand behind the dash clause. Blakemore analyses discourse connectives as carrying **procedural** meaning, encoding instructions that constrain the reader's inference, which is the axis on which the dash is weakest. Nunberg treats punctuation as a grammar in its own right and separates its grammatical role from its presentational one, which is what licenses the exemption for bullets and delimiters. Discourse-relation research, the Penn Discourse Treebank most concretely, divides relations into explicit and implicit by whether a connective signals the sense, which is the imperative the provision states.

The classical objection to the dash is older than any of this: Fowler's complaint was that it serves writers unwilling to take the trouble of choosing the right stop.

## §en2

**Information focus** and its realization by tonic prominence are Halliday's, from the treatment of information structure and the Given and New distinction. The **end-focus** principle, that new information gravitates to the end of the clause, is stated in Quirk and colleagues and descends from the Prague School's functional sentence perspective, where Firbas's **communicative dynamism** grades the contribution each element makes.

Reading bold as a graphological analogue of the tonic is a convention of stylistics rather than a claim Halliday makes. It is sound enough to carry the provision, and it is the reason the remedy can be stated positively: end-focus already marks the New, so a bold span mid-sentence duplicates a signal the structure supplies.

## §en3

The diagnosis is rhetorical structure theory's. Mann and Thompson distinguish nucleus and satellite spans from **multinuclear** relations of equal status, and their Joint relation covers spans held together by nothing beyond adjacency. That is precisely what the reorder test detects.

Rhetorical structure theory supplies the diagnosis; **cohesion** supplies the cure. Halliday and Hasan catalogue the ties that bind a text in sequence, reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion, and the provision's second remedy asks for one of them: a paragraph depends on what the one before it established through a tie of that kind.

The name draws instead on Halliday's parataxis, for continuity with §en1's source. Both terms appear in the provision because "list" alone would read as a claim about typography, and the point is a claim about discourse structure.

## §en4

The weakest grounding of the six, recorded as such so nobody later mistakes it for the order of grounding behind §en5.

Reading a heading as the **Theme** of the section beneath it is a defensible application of Halliday's Theme and Rheme, and it supports treating the heading as a fragment of its section's prose. The name comes from the Chicago Manual, which calls title-case capitalization headline style and its alternative sentence style. No linguistic account condemns headline style, which is a conventional orthographic register with a long history. The provision therefore rests on editorial consistency and on the Theme reading, not on a rule of grammar.

The provision's carve-out for the document title rests on the same reading. An H1 names the document rather than heading a section of it, so no section stands beneath it for the H1 to be the Theme of, and the argument that produces sentence case never engages.

## §en5

The strongest grounding of the six. Halliday's process types make the copula the **congruent** realization of a **relational process**, and **grammatical metaphor** is his term for realizing a meaning through a form typically used for a different one, nominalization being the best-known case. A material-process verb occupying a relational slot is grammatical metaphor by definition.

That definitional footing is why the provision needs no list of offending verbs. The assay is positional: a verb whose lexical content implies action, in a slot where nothing acts.

## §en6

**Metalinguistic negation** is Horn's, for a negation that targets the aptness of a formulation rather than the truth of a proposition. **Accommodation** is Lewis's, for the reader's silent repair of a presupposition the speaker has taken for granted. Together they name both the construction and its cost: the reader must accommodate an interlocutor who asserted the rejected formulation before the claim itself becomes available.

Classical rhetoric's *correctio*, also called epanorthosis, was considered as the provision's name and rejected. It covers "it is not X, it is Y" cleanly but does not reach "not only X but Y", so it would have named two thirds of the target.

# English Lingua Grounding

What follows is the literature behind each `§en` provision, the term as its source uses it, and what the provision deliberately declines to take.

## §en1

The taxonomy is Halliday's **logico-semantic relations**, the system of expansion set out in the chapter on the clause complex: elaboration, extension, enhancement, alongside projection. Elaboration divides into exposition, exemplification and clarification, which is where `i.e.` and `e.g.` enter the mapping under their own names.

Qualifications the provision does not have room for. The mapping is working guidance rather than a reproduction of the taxonomy: the colon is assigned elaboration as a genus while `i.e.` and `e.g.` are assigned two of its species, and "aside" belongs to no category in the system. The list is also partial by necessity, since enhancement has no punctuation realization at all: temporal, causal and conditional relations need a conjunction. A reader who knows the source will notice both and should not take the provision as a claim to reproduce it.

Supporting threads stand behind the dash clause. Blakemore analyses discourse connectives as carrying **procedural** meaning, encoding instructions that constrain the reader's inference, which is the axis on which the dash is weakest. Nunberg treats punctuation as a grammar in its own right and separates its grammatical role from its presentational one, which is what licenses the exemption for bullets and delimiters. Discourse-relation research, the Penn Discourse Treebank most concretely, divides relations into explicit and implicit by whether a connective signals the sense, which is the imperative the provision states.

The classical objection to the dash is older than any of this: Fowler's complaint was that it serves writers unwilling to take the trouble of choosing the right stop.

## §en2

**Information focus** and its realization by tonic prominence are Halliday's, from the treatment of information structure and the Given and New distinction. The **end-focus** principle, that new information gravitates to the end of the clause, is stated in Quirk and colleagues.

Reading bold as a graphological analogue of the tonic is a convention of stylistics rather than a claim Halliday makes. It is sound enough to carry the provision, and it is the reason the remedy can be stated positively: end-focus already marks the New, so a bold span mid-sentence claims a focus the structure withholds, and one on the final element duplicates a signal the structure already supplies.

## §en3

The diagnosis is rhetorical structure theory's. Mann and Thompson distinguish nucleus and satellite spans from **multinuclear** relations of equal status, and their Joint relation covers spans held together by nothing beyond adjacency. That is precisely what the reorder test detects.

The cure is cohesion. Halliday and Hasan catalogue the ties that bind a text as it unfolds (reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion), and the provision's second remedy asks for one of them: a paragraph depends on what the one before it established through a tie of that kind.

For continuity with §en1's source, the name draws instead on Halliday's parataxis. Both terms appear in the provision because "list" alone would read as a claim about typography, and the point is a claim about discourse structure.

## §en4

The weakest grounding of any provision here. It is recorded as such so nobody later mistakes it for the order of grounding behind §en5.

Reading a heading as the Theme of the section beneath it is a defensible application of Halliday's Theme and Rheme, and it supports treating the heading as a fragment of its section's prose. The name is the Chicago Manual's, which calls title-case capitalization headline style and its alternative sentence style. No linguistic account condemns headline style, which is a conventional orthographic register with a long history. The provision therefore rests on editorial consistency and on the Theme reading, not on a rule of grammar.

The provision's carve-out for the document title rests on the same reading. An H1 names the document rather than heading a section of it, so no section stands beneath it for the H1 to be the Theme of, and the argument that produces sentence case never engages.

## §en5

The strongest grounding of any provision here. In Halliday's process types the copula is the congruent realization of a relational process, and **grammatical metaphor** is his term for realizing a meaning through a form typically used for a different one. A material-process verb occupying a relational slot is grammatical metaphor by definition.

That definitional footing is why the provision needs no list of offending verbs. The assay is positional: a verb whose lexical content implies action, in a slot where nothing acts.

## §en6

**Metalinguistic negation** is Horn's, for a negation that targets the aptness of a formulation rather than the truth of a proposition. **Accommodation** is Lewis's, for the reader's silent repair of a presupposition the speaker has taken for granted. Together they name both the construction and its cost: the reader must accommodate an interlocutor who asserted the rejected formulation before the claim itself becomes available.

Classical rhetoric's *correctio*, also called epanorthosis, was considered as the provision's name and rejected. It covers "it is not X, it is Y" cleanly but does not reach "not only X but Y", so it would have named the first of those and missed the second.

## §en7

The construction is Dik's **Tail**, the extra-clausal constituent at his P3 position, and the afterthought is his own gloss for it: material outside the clause, added after it, to clarify or modify what the clause has already said. Position does not define it, since Dik separates the Tail from the Tag at that same position. The name takes his word because "appositive" names a relation where the provision needs a position.

Prosody is what separates speech from prose. An extra-clausal constituent sits outside the clause's prosodic integration, so a spoken Tail is set off in a contour of its own, which tells the listener at the moment it begins that an afterthought is coming. Prose has no such channel: the element arrives inside the same orthographic sentence, indistinguishable from clause-internal content, and the reader has spent on it before learning what it was. Sperber and Wilson's **optimal relevance** names that spend, effects weighed against the effort of processing. Chekhov's gun is the same intuition in narrative form.

What follows is the provision's own rather than the literature's. No account of the Tail states the undeveloped-promise assay, which is a claim about a reader's expectations. The extension to a heading or caption rests on §en4's reading of a heading as the Theme of the section beneath it, an extrapolation that entry already records as one.
